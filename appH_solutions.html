Appendix H  Solutions to Exercises

These are worked solutions to the end-of-chapter exercises. Coding exercises that overlap with a lab point to the reference solution.py in that lab rather than repeating the code. For the measurement exercises, the solution describes the outcome the cost model predicts and how to check it; your own numbers will depend on your hardware.

Chapter 1: The Landscape

Exercise 1.1

By the definition of conditional probability, \(p(x_1,\dots,x_T)=p(x_T\mid x_{<T})\,p(x_1,\dots,x_{T-1})\); applying this recursively down to \(p(x_1)\) gives the product. No modelling assumption is involved, so an exact autoregressive model can represent any distribution. With a causal mask, the output at position \(t\) depends only on \(x_{\le t}\), so position \(t\) is a valid prediction of \(x_{t+1}\) for every \(t\): one forward pass over \(T\) tokens yields \(T\) supervised predictions (the last uses the next token in the stream). A masked LM computes a loss only at the \(\approx0.15T\) masked positions, because unmasked positions can see their own answer through bidirectional attention.

Exercise 1.3

Wikipedia: \(0.03\times300\text{B}=9\text{B}\) tokens sampled from a 3B-token source, about 3 epochs. Common Crawl: \(0.60\times300\text{B}=180\text{B}\) from 410B, about 0.44 epochs. Exercise 1.4

\(C=64\times30\times86{,}400\,\text{s}\times989\times10^{12}\times0.35\approx5.74\times10^{22}\) FLOPs. With \(C=6ND\) and \(D=20N\), \(C=120N^2\), so \(N=\sqrt{C/120}\approx2.2\times10^{10}\) (about 22B parameters) and \(D\approx4.4\times10^{11}\) tokens. If the model will be served heavily, total cost is training plus \(2N\) FLOPs per served token. Shrinking \(N\) (say to 7B) and spending the same compute on more tokens (\(D=C/(6N)\approx1.4\)T) gives a slightly worse model at equal compute but a \(3\times\) cheaper one to serve, which is usually the better trade.

Exercise 1.5

bf16: weights \(4.02\times10^9\times2=8.04\) GB, KV at 4,096 tokens \(=147{,}456\times4{,}096=0.60\) GB, total 8.64 GB, giving \(8.64/3.35\times10^{12}\approx2.58\) ms, or about 387 tokens/s. int4 weights: 2.01 GB + 0.60 GB = 2.61 GB, about 0.78 ms, or about 1,280 tokens/s. Weights still dominate, but the KV share of traffic rises from 7% to 23%; at longer contexts or larger batches the KV cache becomes the dominant term, which motivates KV-cache quantisation (Chapter 48).

Exercise 1.6

Per step, compute time is \(B\cdot2N/F\) and memory time is \(Nb/W\) (weights read once, shared by the batch). Equating gives \(B^\star=bF/(2W)\). For an H100 in bf16, \(B^\star=2\times989/(2\times3.35)\approx295\), matching the lab’s 296–320 (the lab includes the LM head and attention FLOPs and embedding bytes, which shift the value slightly). With long contexts each sequence adds its own KV bytes, which are not shared across the batch. Attention over the cache has an operational intensity of \(H/G\) FLOP/byte in bf16 (3.6): 2 for Qwen3-0.6B and 1.7B, 4 for Qwen3-4B and 8B, regardless of batch size. That is two orders of magnitude below the ridge, so the step needs a larger batch, or never becomes compute-bound when KV traffic dominates.

Exercise 1.7

Exercise 1.9

Example probes: (i) “All squares have five sides. ABCD is a square. How many sides does ABCD have?” (closed-world answer 5; a good reasoner flags the false premise). (ii) A freshly generated puzzle with random names and quantities, for instance a scheduling constraint problem produced by a script with a computed answer. (iii) A question whose answer runs against frequency, for instance “Which is heavier, a kilogram of feathers or 900 g of steel?”. Base models often continue the text without answering; instruction-tuned models answer fluently but fall for (iii) more often; reasoning models do best on (ii). Score automatically with programmatic answers and the verifier of Chapter 56, and for (i) with a rubric for “flags the inconsistency” judged by an LLM (Chapter 56).

Exercise 1.10

A reasonable ranking is distillation, then RL, then inference-time scaling. Strict latency rules out heavy per-query sampling. One week of compute is enough to fine-tune on traces from a stronger teacher, which is cheap and stable, and the fine-tuned model serves at its normal speed. RL is attractive if a reliable automatic reward exists (for instance, review comments that can be checked against later bug fixes) but is costlier to make work; it can follow distillation as a second stage. A light form of inference-time scaling (one short chain of thought) can still be used if the latency budget allows.

Exercise 1.12

Prediction (report.py --hw A100-40GB-PCIe or decode_step_roofline directly): at a context of about 160 tokens (the 32-token prompt plus half of 256 new tokens used by the benchmark) a decode step moves 1.21 GB, so the bound is 0.78 ms per token (1,285 tokens/s) on the A100-40GB PCIe and 0.36 ms (2,768 tokens/s) on the H100; at 1,024 tokens of context, 0.84 ms and 0.39 ms. The ratio, 2.15, is the bandwidth ratio \(3.35/1.555\), because both are bandwidth-bound. The critical batch is 201 on the A100 and 296 on the H100, the ratio of the ridge points (1.47), not of the bandwidths.

Measurement (labs/lab45_inference/bench.py --exp compile --device cuda --size 0.6B, measured on 2x A100-40GB (PCIe), torch 2.14.1): eager decoding runs at 29.8 tokens/s (33.6 ms per token, 43 times the bound) and torch.compile at 162 tokens/s (6.2 ms, 8 times the bound); the reduce-overhead mode reaches 153 tokens/s because Inductor skips CUDA graphs when the KV cache is mutated in place. Eager decoding of a 0.6B model is regime (b) of Figure 3.6: several hundred small kernels per token, each paying Python and launch overhead, so the step is bound by the host, not by either roof. That also answers the question about ratios: the predicted ratio of critical batches is a property of the GPUs and would hold once batches are large, but the measured batch-1 speed ratio between an A100 and an H100 would be close to 1 in eager mode (the same host does the same work) and would approach 2.15 only as compilation, CUDA graphs and a lean decode loop remove the overhead.

Chapter 2: PyTorch Essentials

Exercise 2.1

Contiguous \((2,3,4)\) has strides \((12,4,1)\). After transpose(0, 2) the shape is \((4,3,2)\) and the strides are \((1,4,12)\), over the same storage. .view(-1) requires that the flattened order equal the storage order, which no longer holds, so it raises an error. .reshape(-1) falls back to a copy: one kernel that reads and writes all 24 elements (in general, \(2\times\) the tensor’s bytes of traffic plus a new allocation).

Exercise 2.2

bf16 has 7 explicit mantissa bits, so the spacing of representable numbers in \([1,2)\) is \(2^{-7}=0.0078125\). With round-to-nearest-even, \(1+2^{-8}\) rounds back to 1, so any \(\delta\) slightly larger than \(2^{-8}\approx0.0039\) is needed to change the value. An update of \(10^{-4}\) is lost entirely: the weight never moves. fp32 master weights have spacing \(2^{-23}\approx1.2\times10^{-7}\) near 1, so the update is applied exactly in fp32 and the bf16 copy is re-derived from the master after each step; small updates accumulate until they cross a bf16 rounding boundary.

Exercise 2.3

With \(a=\sigma(z)\) and \(\mathcal{L}=-[y\log a+(1-y)\log(1-a)]\): \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a = -y/a + (1-y)/(1-a) = (a-y)/(a(1-a))\) and \(\partial a/\partial z=a(1-a)\), so \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z=a-y\). For a batch with mean loss, \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}}=\frac1N\mathbf{X}^\top(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{y})\) and \(\partial/\partial b=\frac1N\sum_i(a_i-y_i)\). This is exactly logistic_regression_grads (Task 3) in the lab solution, which matches autograd to \(10^{-6}\).

Exercise 2.4

  1. The Jacobian is \(m\times n\). Forward mode builds it one column at a time: each Jacobian–vector product pushes one tangent through every layer, and for the linear layers that costs one matrix product the size of the forward one, so \(n\) columns cost about \(n\) forward passes plus the primal pass, \((1+n)F\). Reverse mode builds it one row at a time: each vector–Jacobian product with respect to the input needs only \(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{W}\) per layer (no weight gradient), again one forward-sized product, so \(m\) rows cost about \((1+m)F\), with the primal pass recorded once. (b) With layers \(n\to1024\to1024\to m\), one forward pass is \(2(1024n+1024^2+1024m)\) FLOPs, 3.2 MFLOP for \((512, 8)\). Forward mode costs about \(513F\approx1.6\) GFLOP and reverse mode about \(9F\approx29\) MFLOP, so in FLOPs jacrev should be about 57 times cheaper for \((512, 8)\) and jacfwd about 57 times cheaper for \((8, 512)\). (c) Expect the measured ratio on a GPU to be far smaller than 57. Both functions vectorise their passes with vmap, so the 512 tangents (or cotangents) become one matrix product per layer that reads each weight once: the expensive mode is a GEMM with \(M=512\), at a much higher operational intensity than the matrix–vector products of the cheap mode, which are bound by memory and by kernel launches (Chapter 3). The cheap mode cannot go below a fixed cost of a few dozen launches, and the expensive one runs its extra FLOPs at a far better rate, so the time ratio is a small factor. On a CPU, where the GEMMs are compute-bound at much smaller sizes, the ratio moves towards the FLOP ratio. The direction never changes: with few outputs, use reverse mode.

Exercise 2.5

  1. Per block the saved tensors become the block input (saved by the first nn.Linear for its weight gradient) and \(\mathbf{U}\); \(\mathbf{W}_2\) is a parameter and is not counted. With 8,192 tokens, \(d=4096\), hidden 16,384 and bf16, that is \(8192\times(4096+16384)\times2 = 335.5\) MB per block and 5.37 GB for 16 blocks, against 9.66 GB without the function and 1.07 GB with full checkpointing. saved_activation_bytes reports exactly \(16\times335{,}544{,}320\) bytes, because it counts the tensors the hooks see and deduplicates by data pointer. (b) The backward pass now recomputes one GELU per element of \(\mathbf{U}\), about a dozen FLOPs for each of \(8192\times16384\) elements, roughly 1.6 GFLOP per block, against 6.6 TFLOP of GEMM work in the block’s forward and backward: negligible. The bytes matter more: reading \(\mathbf{U}\) and writing \(\operatorname{GELU}(\mathbf{U})\) moves 537 MB per block, about 0.4 ms at 1,369 GB/s, or about 6 ms for the stack, roughly 1% of the 501 ms step (a kernel that applies GELU while loading the second GEMM’s operand avoids even that write). Full checkpointing instead recomputes both GEMMs, 2.2 TFLOP and about 10 ms per block at 217 TFLOP/s. The lean block saves 4.3 GB for about 1% of the step; full checkpointing saves 8.6 GB for a third more FLOPs. Recomputing cheap, memory-bound operations is the idea of selective recomputation (Korthikanti et al., 2022).

Exercise 2.8

  1. The step consumes \(32\times2048=65{,}536\) tokens every 0.25 s, 262,144 tokens/s. One worker produces a batch in \(65{,}536/400{,}000=164\) ms, faster than the 250 ms step, so one worker keeps up with 1.5\(\times\) headroom; two give room for jitter. The loader delivers batches in order, assigning them to workers round-robin, so a stalled worker delays the batch it owes even if the other worker is idle. With two workers each owes every second batch, one per 0.5 s, and a 1 s stall is hidden only if that worker already has two finished batches queued: prefetch_factor of at least 2 (the default), better 3, which is 4 to 6 batches, 1–1.5 s of steps, in flight. (b) Stored as uint16, the loader reads \(262{,}144\times2=0.5\) MB/s, nothing for a disk or the page cache. What limits it is per-batch Python work (indexing, building an int64 tensor, passing it from the worker through shared memory), on the order of a millisecond per batch, still far below 250 ms. (c) The inputs are \(65{,}536\times8=524\) KB of int64 (the shifted targets as many again), about 26 μs each at 20 GB/s, negligible next to the step. non_blocking=True matters for another reason: a blocking copy makes the CPU wait until the copy has run, and the copy runs only after every kernel already queued on the stream, so the CPU stops running ahead of the GPU and the GPU then idles while the next step’s kernels are launched (Section 3.7).

Exercise 2.9

Gradients are 14 GB in bf16. A ring all-reduce sends and receives \(2(P-1)/P\times14\approx24.5\) GB per GPU for \(P=8\). Over NVLink at 300 GB/s this takes \(\approx82\) ms, easily hidden behind a 1.5 s step. Over 100 Gb/s Ethernet (12.5 GB/s) it takes \(\approx2.0\) s, longer than the compute, so communication cannot be hidden and the step at least doubles. The model does not fit for DDP anyway: 7B parameters \(\times\) 16 bytes \(=112\) GB of weights, gradients and Adam state exceeds 80 GB before activations. This requires ZeRO/FSDP sharding (Chapter 24).

Exercise 2.10

  1. The gradients are \(350\times10^6\times2=700\) MB. With \(P=2\), a ring all-reduce sends \(2(P-1)/P=1\) times the gradient volume per GPU in each direction, 700 MB, so the transfers alone take \(700/20=35\) ms, well under the 120 ms backward pass. With \(n=700/B\) buckets of \(B\) MB, assume gradients become ready at a uniform rate, one bucket every \(120/n\) ms, and that the all-reduces run one after another, each taking \(\alpha+B/\beta\). If each all-reduce is shorter than the interval between buckets, communication keeps up and only the last bucket, which becomes ready when backward ends, is exposed: \(\alpha+B/\beta\). Otherwise a backlog builds and the exposed time is about \(n(\alpha+B/\beta)-120\) ms. Taking \(\alpha=0.1\) ms as an assumption to be measured: \(B=1\) MB gives 700 calls of 0.15 ms against an interval of 0.17 ms, so it barely keeps up and exposes about 0.15 ms, but any \(\alpha\) above 0.12 ms tips it into a backlog (at \(\alpha=0.2\) ms, \(700\times0.25-120=55\) ms exposed); \(B=25\) MB exposes about 1.35 ms; \(B=100\) MB about 5.1 ms; \(B=1{,}000\) MB holds all 700 MB in one bucket that starts only when backward ends, exposing the full 35 ms. The prediction is a broad optimum between a few and a few tens of megabytes, with small buckets punished by \(\alpha\) and large ones by lost overlap. (b) Measure the step with torch.cuda.Event timing after a warm-up, for each bucket_cap_mb, and check once that both ranks hold identical weights after a step, since on this box direct peer copies are unreliable while NCCL is correct. (c) The assumptions to check first: gradients are not produced uniformly (DDP orders buckets in reverse parameter order, and its first bucket is small by design); NCCL’s kernels use SMs and slow the backward pass they overlap with; with no peer-to-peer path, NCCL stages through host memory, so \(\beta\) may be well below 20 GB/s; and \(\alpha\) includes the CPU cost of launching each call, which competes with the backward pass’s own launches.

Exercise 3.2

Exercise 3.3

By Little’s law the H100 needs \(3.35\times10^{12}\times500\times10^{-9}\approx1.68\) MB of loads in flight, \(1.68\times10^6/132\approx12.7\) KB per SM, about 25 outstanding warp loads of 512 bytes per SM at every moment. The A100 needs 6.3 KB, about a dozen. Per SM the H100 must therefore keep twice as many bytes in flight, while the per-SM resources that hold them grew far less: the register file is the same 256 KB and shared memory went from 164 KB to 228 KB. A kernel whose occupancy and per-warp parallelism were just enough on the A100 keeps roughly half of the bytes in flight that the H100 needs, and reaches roughly half of its bandwidth roof, with no change in its code. This is one reason Hopper added the Tensor Memory Accelerator, which issues large asynchronous bulk copies without occupying a thread per load (??).

Exercise 3.4

  1. \(\lceil1408/128\rceil^2=11^2=121\) output tiles; with one tile per SM at a time, 108 SMs need \(\lceil121/108\rceil=2\) waves, and the second wave has 13 tiles, so 12% of its SM-time does useful work. (b) The GEMM occupies \(2\times108=216\) tile-slots for 121 tiles of work, so quantisation alone caps it at \(121/216=56\%\) of the compute roof (before the fixed costs of Section 3.6). (c) In the sweep’s range the tile count steps with \(\lceil n/128\rceil\): \(n\le1{,}280\) gives 100 tiles in one wave (93% of the slots); \(1{,}281\le n\le1{,}408\) gives 121 tiles in two waves (56%); \(1{,}409\le n\le1{,}536\) gives 144 tiles in two waves (67%). Expect a sawtooth: throughput falls sharply between 1,280 and 1,312, where the work grows by 5% and the time almost doubles, and recovers partly towards 1,536. In practice cuBLAS picks among tile shapes and may split the \(K\) dimension across SMs for shapes like these, which smooths the steps; how much of the sawtooth survives tells you how well its heuristics cover the shape.

Exercise 3.5

For 1,024 elements, both eager and compiled versions are dominated by per-op overhead: eager time is about \(100\times\) the per-launch cost (hundreds of μs to milliseconds); this is regime (b) of Figure 3.6. For \(10^8\) elements, each op is a memory-bound kernel taking \(\approx0.24\) ms in fp32 on an H100, so eager time is about 24 ms and overhead is hidden; this is regime (a). torch.compile fuses the chain into one kernel that reads and writes once. The large tensor speeds up by up to \(\approx100\times\) in traffic terms, and the small tensor speeds up because 100 launches became one; this is regime (c). With \(s=\max|x|/q_{\max}\) the rounding error of each element inside the range is uniform on \([-s/2,s/2]\) (5.4), so the MSE over the elements sharing a scale is \(s^2/12= \max|x|^2/(12q_{\max}^2)\), and averaged over blocks it is \(\mathbb{E}[\max|x|^2]/(12q_{\max}^2)\). Exercise 7.2.

With \(p=\operatorname{softmax}(z_t)\) and \(L=-\frac1N\sum_t\ln p_{t,y_t}\), \(\partial L/\partial z_{t,j}=\frac1N(p_{t,j}-\mathbb{1}[j=y_t])\), the usual softmax–cross-entropy gradient divided by the number of scored tokens. Near the floor, a context’s eight possible successors have probabilities 0.37 down to 0.05, so their logit gradients are \(0.05/N\) to \(0.37/N\) for the non-target successors and up to \((1-p)/N\approx0.95/N\) in magnitude for the target, i.e. \(2.5\times10^{-5}\) to \(4.8\times10^{-4}\) for \(N\approx2{,}000\) (\(2^{-15.3}\) to \(2^{-11}\)): normal in FP16 above \(2^{-14}\) and subnormal below; in E5M2 the same split (its smallest normal is also \(2^{-14}\)), with only one or two significant bits at the bottom; in E4M3 (smallest subnormal \(2^{-9}\)) all zero without a scale. The other 24 tokens of the vocabulary have probabilities that a trained model drives towards zero, \(10^{-4}\) and below, giving gradients of \(5\times10^{-8}\) and less. FP16 does not flush \(5.0\times10^{-8}\): anything above \(2^{-25}\) rounds up to the smallest subnormal \(2^{-24}=5.96\times10^{-8}\) (here a 19% error); only below \(p\approx5\times10^{-5}\), i.e. gradients under \(2.98\times10^{-8}\), does FP16 flush to zero. E5M2 and E4M3 flush all of them. The median over all 32 logits is therefore one of these tiny values. The step-51 histogram of Figure 7.4 (all GEMM inputs, not only logits) peaks at \(2^{-20}\), inside FP16’s subnormal range and below both FP8 ranges, which is why fp8_noscale flushes most gradients and fp16_noscale keeps most of them with reduced precision.

For the GPT-2-sized run, a plausible token with \(p\approx1/300\) gives \(3.3\times10^{-3}/8{,}192=4\times10^{-7}\approx2^{-21.2}\): an FP16 subnormal with about three significant bits, zero in E5M2 and E4M3. The tens of thousands of implausible tokens, with \(p\lesssim10^{-6}\), give \(\lesssim1.2\times10^{-10}\), zero even in FP16; they are the median. Larger vocabularies and larger batches push the whole distribution down, which is why loss scaling, or BF16, is indispensable at scale, and why FP8 gradients need per-tensor or finer scales.

Exercise 7.3.

The largest gradient in the last histogram is below \(2^{-8}\). That bound guarantees \(S\max|g|<2^{16}\) for \(S=2^{24}\), which is not enough: FP16 overflows at \(65{,}520<2^{16}\), so a gradient just under \(2^{-8}\) would overflow. The histogram guarantees safety for \(S\le2^{23}\) (\(S\max|g|<2^{15}\)). At \(S=2^{23}\) only values below \(2^{-47}\) would still flush, about 1.7% of the nonzero values, all of them far too small to matter. GradScaler does not compute \(S\) from a histogram because the histogram costs a pass over every gradient and a host synchronisation per step, the maximum changes during training (it grew by 2 binades between our first and last histograms), and an overflow is cheap to detect after the fact (one isfinite reduction) and cheap to recover from (skip one step, halve \(S\)). Starting high and backing off finds the largest safe scale in a few skipped steps and tracks it afterwards.

Exercise 7.4.

With history amax 1.0 the scale is \(448\). At the spike every element with \(|x|\ge(448+16)/448\approx1.036\) saturates to \(448/448 = 1\), so all elements between 1.036 and 8 are clipped and the largest is reduced by a factor of 8 (to 1.0). The spike’s own amax enters the history after the cast, so the next step is scaled correctly (\(448/8=56\)). But the history keeps the maximum of the last \(L\) amax values, so for the following \(L\) steps the ordinary tensors (amax 1) are scaled by 56 instead of 448: they use only the bottom part of E4M3’s range, and every value below \(2^{-9}/56\approx3.5\times10^{-5}\) flushes to zero instead of below \(4.4\times10^{-6}\); three binades of range are lost for \(L\) steps. A margin \(\mu=1\) halves the scale on every step: the top binade of E4M3 is never used, the underflow threshold rises by a factor of 2, and relative precision of normal values is unchanged (it is set by the 3 fraction bits). A spike of \(8\times\) still saturates with \(\mu=1\); it takes \(\mu=3\) to absorb it. test_delayed_scaling_uses_previous_amax is the \(4\times\) version of this experiment.

Exercise 7.5.

At \(|w|=0.02\) the BF16 gap is \(q=2^{-13}=1.22\times10^{-4}\) and an update of \(10^{-5}\) is the fraction \(f=0.082\) of it. Each step, stochastic rounding moves the weight by \(q\) with probability \(f\) and by 0 otherwise: mean \(10^{-5}\) (unbiased), variance \(q^2f(1-f)=1.1\times10^{-9}\), standard deviation \(3.3\times10^{-5}\). After \(T\) steps the signal is \(T\cdot10^{-5}\) and the noise \(\sqrt T\cdot3.3\times 10^{-5}\); the signal dominates after \(T\approx11\) steps and the relative noise falls as \(3.3/\sqrt T\): 10% after about 1,100 steps. So stochastic rounding behaves like a small extra gradient noise whose size is set by the ulp, and the optimiser is already averaging much larger minibatch noise. In Table 7.4 bf16_pure_sr sits at +0.04 on the transformer and +0.02 on the MLP, inside single-seed noise; the full study’s three seeds will say whether any residual gap is real.

Exercise 7.6.

In \(\partial L/\partial W = (\partial L/\partial Y)^\top X\) the sum runs over tokens: entry \((i,j)\) is \(\sum_t g_{t,i}x_{t,j}\). A block-scaled operand contributes \(s_b\,\tilde{x}_{t,j}\), and the scale can be pulled out of a partial sum only if it is constant over the summation index within the block. So the scales of \(X\) must be constant over runs of tokens, i.e. blocks along the token dimension; the same for \(\partial L/\partial Y\). Blocking \(X\) along \(d_{\text{in}}\) instead gives each \((t,j)\) a scale that varies with \(t\) inside one dot product, and an MX tensor core, which multiplies codes and applies one scale pair per \(K\)-block, cannot compute that product: the fake-quantised version is a computation no hardware performs. Its error is not necessarily larger. With the activations’ outliers concentrated in a few channels, blocks along \(d_{\text{in}}\) group the outlier channel with ordinary ones in every token, while blocks along tokens keep each channel’s values together. Measured with MXFP4 on a \(512\)-token layer (\(d_{\text{in}}=256\), \(d_{\text{out}}=128\)): with Gaussian activations both orientations give a weight-gradient error of 16.2%; with four outlier channels (20 times larger) the token orientation gives 16.0% and the \(d_{\text{in}}\) orientation 18.2%. The exercise’s point is that “which orientation is more accurate” is not the question; only one is implementable.
